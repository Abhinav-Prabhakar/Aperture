Research memo24 November 2026

What researchers actually need

The first eighteen instruments proved that a research design system can exist. They did not yet prove why it needs to. This is the audit that changed the catalog: what the evidence says, the six kinds of state generic UI has no representation for, and the eight instruments built from that finding.

The audit

Eighteen instruments sounds like a lot until each one is asked what research invariant it encodes. Eight are generic patterns with research styling. Three are real measurement primitives. Seven are domain or workflow views. None of the eighteen record what happens between acquisition and publication.

CountWhat it really isInstruments
8Generic patternsStat, Sparkline, Timeline, Status Badge, Log Viewer, Metadata Editor, File Dropzone, Data Table.
3Measurement primitivesScientific Number, Quantity Input, Uncertainty Input. These already encode a unit, a precision, or an error.
7Domain or workflow viewsSequence Viewer, Growth Curve, Dose–Response, Spectrum Plot, Periodic Table, Constants Reference, Protocol Steps.
0Research-act recordsNothing recorded what was excluded, calibrated, compared, approved, or used to produce a figure.

The count is not the problem; the semantic density is. A competent team can build eight of these in a sprint. A research team cannot build the missing layer without domain rules, standards, and edge cases. That is what a registry should package.

What the evidence says

The memo is desk research: peer-reviewed studies of data practice and reproducibility, reporting standards, and a review of adjacent libraries. No primary interviews were conducted. The numbers below are the ones that shaped the build.

ProblemReported rateWhy it matters
Spreadsheet identifiers30.9% of papers with Excel gene lists3,436 papers, 5,086 files; up from 19.6% in the original scan. Generic tables still coerce SEPT2 into a date.
Notebook reproducibility24.11% executed; 4.03% reproducedA large Jupyter study. Hidden state and out-of-order cells are the norm, not the exception.
Error bars49% SE · 42% SD · 5% unspecified · one 95% CIA review of Nature Methods articles. The bar exists; the contract for what it means does not.
Reporting complianceRandomization 30–40%; blinding ~20%; sample size <10%ARRIVE 2.0. The decisions were made months before the checklist was opened.
Sample identity4 misidentifications in 284 isolations (1.4%)One biobank, with SOPs in place. The failure was a label, an aliquot, or a handoff.
Data handling84% of 286 researchers; 14% had a LIMSA mixed-method study. Spreadsheets remain the lab database, and the backlog is measured in person-hours.

Read together, the failures are not statistical. They are record-keeping failures with statistical consequences: a typed identifier became a date; a run was never repeated outside one machine; an error bar had no stated meaning; a checklist was reconstructed after the fact; a sample label was lost at a handoff. A chart library cannot prevent any of them.

Six kinds of state

This taxonomy is the real product architecture. It applies in life sciences, physical sciences, social sciences, and computational research, which is why the instruments are not filed under a domain pack.

StateThe question it answersBuilt instrument
IdentityWhat is this, exactly?Dataset Card
ProvenanceWhere did it come from, and what happened to it?Exclusion Ledger, Reproducibility Manifest
UncertaintyHow sure are we, and what is missing?Measurement Table, Missingness Map, Statistical Result
DispositionWhat did we decide to do with it?Exclusion Ledger, Calibration & QC
ComparabilityCan these be compared?Measurement Table, Run Comparison
ComplianceDoes it meet the standard?Calibration & QC, Reproducibility Manifest

The test

A candidate is an instrument only if it scores well on all six criteria. Score each 0–2. Shortlist at 9/12; build at 10/12 with a clear owner.

  1. Object or act. It represents a research object — sample, dataset, run, figure — or a research act: exclude, calibrate, compare, approve, publish.
  2. Prevents a known error. It blocks a documented failure mode: unit mismatch, silent exclusion, identifier corruption, uncorrected p-values, lost metadata.
  3. Cross-domain. It is useful in at least three disciplines, or two domain packs plus a computational workflow.
  4. Not generic. A competent team cannot build it correctly from a generic UI kit without domain rules or standards.
  5. Copy-paste sized. One compound component or a small family, with no backend required for the demo.
  6. Produces an artifact. CSV, JSON, manifest, audit trail, figure, or submission-ready text — not just a view.

The companion rule: an instrument must compose at least three atoms and enforce at least one research invariant. If it can be assembled from elements in an afternoon without domain knowledge, it is an element, not an instrument.

What was built

The first pack tells one story: from instrument file to a comparable, documented dataset without losing the evidence. Seven are Ready; Calibration & QC stays in Preview until a laboratory advisor reviews its defaults.

What changed in the catalog

  • Generic patterns stay in the catalog, but the memo names them for what they are. Eight of the founding eighteen are patterns; only the research records are held to the twelve-point test.
  • The research-record group sits beside the domain packs, not inside them. A Measurement Table is the same instrument in a biochemistry lab, a materials lab, and a survey archive.
  • The build order is by leverage, not pipeline order: the table first, then the ledger, then calibration, then comparison, then identity. The workbench recipes in the memo show how the pieces compose.
  • The count is no longer the headline. A semantic layer that records identity, provenance, uncertainty, disposition, comparability, and compliance is worth more than another chart.

What is not built

The memo lists twenty instruments. This pack is eight of them, deliberately the ones that carry the most evidence per line of code. Still ahead: Import Mapper, Sample Lineage, Run Card, Provenance Graph, Review & Curation Queue, Inter-rater Agreement, Reporting Checklist, Preregistration Diff, Screening Flow, Plate Layout, Ontology Picker, and Figure Spec. Each has a contract and a score in the full memo.
